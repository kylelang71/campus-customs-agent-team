Why the purchase did not change the cash balance

  • It was a purchase order, not a payment. Approval 4 placed an order with Bulldog Print Co for 12 hoodies. The system records an approved order as a new open invoice (invoice 502, $264.00, due 2026-09-05): a bill the shop now owes. It does not write a payments row and does not touch cash_accounts, so no cash changed hands when it was approved.
  • Cash leaves checking only when a payment is approved. Approvals 1 and 3 were payments: each wrote a payments row, deducted the amount from checking, and either moved the lease's next due date (rent) or marked the invoice paid (invoice 501). Paying invoice 502 later would be a separate payment approval.
  • So the purchase changed what the shop owes, not what it holds. Checking was $1,000.00 before and after approval 4. The $264.00 is a liability waiting to be paid.
  • The cash rule still applied to the purchase. Approving an order is blocked if its amount is more than the current checking balance. $264.00 against $1,000.00 passed.

What is still open

  • Invoice 502, $264.00, due 2026-09-05, unpaid. Checking is $160.00, so paying it would be blocked until cash covers it (cash cannot go negative; the model has no incoming cash). The shortfall is $104.00.
  • Order of approvals mattered. The $840.00 payment was still pending when the $264.00 order was approved, so the two together ($1,104.00) were more than the $1,000.00 on hand. The purchase check compares the order with the balance at that moment, not with other pending items. Had the payment been approved first (checking $160.00), the order would have been blocked.
  • Rent next due 2026-10-02, $2,400.00.

1. How would you evaluate the performance of the agents on each ticket, and why?

Performance was best on ticket 102, and equally weaker on tickets 101 and 103.

  • Ticket 102 (best). The objective was relatively straightforward and the pings between agents were minimal.
  • Tickets 101 and 103 (weaker, about equal). There were redundant pings between agents, and the Accounting model seemed to time out relatively frequently.
  • Approvals. Early on, the tickets asked for approval of a combined payment and message. In the later stages of refinement the customer messages and the payments or purchases were sent for approval separately, although that was not explicitly requested.

What the audit trail shows.

Handoffs and check-ins per run across all test runs: ticket 101 averaged 8.8 delegate calls and 3.5 check-ins (its worst run had 29 calls and 27 check-ins, before the Boss started passing its notes on), ticket 102 averaged 5.7 and 2.3, ticket 103 averaged 4.2 and 1.6. In the final runs: 101 had 7 calls and 1 check-in, 102 had 5 and 2, 103 had 5 and 2.

Accounting accounts for the most long silences: 19 of the 51 gaps over 20 seconds, partly because it makes the most model calls (159 steps, tied with the Boss). The longest stalls came right after it prepared a payment or order (58 to 157 seconds when a typed reply was requested), and in the final run the Boss had to check in on Accounting on all three tickets.

Each payment, purchase order and customer message is its own approval, decided separately (5 approvals of 3 kinds in the final run). The customer-message approval was added in Problem 9 after the request for an approval process for customer service messages.

2. For each ticket, how did Actual compare to the Expected plan?

I was right about the initial agent calls but wrong about the complexity and the amount of back and forth it would take. Before I finalized the design I added a step where the Boss shares all the notes it has collected for a given ticket with the agents it asks next. That cut the excessive, redundant and repeated tool use by individual agents trying to fact-find things that had already been looked up. Once that was fixed, the behavior was relatively as expected.

3. What would have been simpler as one agent with tools, and why?

The Facilities, Accounting and Inventory agents could have been collapsed into one general database-management agent with access to all the tools, for reading and writing the database. The accounting function of writing and the customer service function of writing were appropriate as separate functions. I really liked that the Boss had a robust review back and forth with each of them, because they handle very serious material, and I would want that in a commercial setting as well.

Why it would be simpler: Facilities' whole job was one read_lease call per ticket (about 7 seconds inside a run of nearly four minutes), and much of the Boss's work went into passing what one agent had learned to the next so nobody asked twice. A single agent holding all the read tools would not need that.

4. Three new problems Campus Customs might face that this agent team could solve with the tools built

  1. Drafting pricing negotiations with a vendor. Inventory or Accounting drafts the message and the Boss reviews it before it is final, using the vendor and invoice tools.
  2. Pricing different clothes, each with a different cost of goods sold, for a bulk order. Accounting reads each item's unit cost and list price and commits to a price above the 30% margin floor, as it did with the hoodies ($49).
  3. Answering questions about ordering timelines and which payment should go out first. Vendor lead times and open invoices come from the database, and the Boss's cash outlook and payment review decide the order of payments.

5. Three new problems this agent team could not solve with the tools built, and what it would need

  1. Customer interaction. This needs a chat window and new database or note-taking fields so that recent conversations can be parsed by the agents. The customer-facing chat would need its own website (like HW4) and would land on the customer-facing site. Today Customer Service has no route to customers: it only drafts through the Boss.
  2. Human review loops. This needs the same kind of chat window and conversation fields. The user chat could feasibly be added to the desk interface, between the Boss agent and the user. Today a person can only approve or decline a prepared item, with no way to ask a follow-up or give a reason.
  3. Proactive ordering when inventory is low. For agents to create tickets on their own, such as inventory supplementation, there would have to be a persistent background heartbeat so that checks run, say, every hour or at the start of every day. A cloud partner could provide that service. Today a run starts only when a person presses Start.

I envision these features coming from other places than new tools and new agents: a chat window and conversation fields (user to Boss, customer to Customer Service) and a background scheduler, feeding the agents already built.
